FormFill · AI model selection

Which AI model reads your forms, and what it costs

An overview of the model selection process behind form classification, and the tradeoffs between the available options.

00

Summary

Model testing proceeded in three phases: local inference on consumer hardware, free-tier cloud models, and paid cloud models. Each phase used real form PDFs, not synthetic test data. Full results and methodology for each phase are documented below; this table summarises the headline finding from each.

Phase Scope Result
1. Local (Ollama) 7 models narrowed to 4, evaluated on 2 real forms gemma3:4b, qwen2.5vl:7b, and gemma4:26b (the mentor's recommendation) all produced correct results. Consumer hardware without a dedicated GPU made this impractically slow for production (5–20 minutes per form).
2. Cloud, free tier 3 free OpenRouter models, evaluated on 16 real forms with individual retesting Only google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free proved reliable — fast (seconds per form) and accurate whenever it succeeded, with one confirmed limitation: consistent failures on official NZ government forms specifically. The other two free models never returned a usable result.
3. Cloud, paid tier 7 paid OpenRouter models, including the mentor's original recommendation, benchmarked against the free-tier result on 5 real forms Paid models were 15–35× faster than local inference at a cost of a few cents per form, and confirmed to resolve the free tier's government-form limitation. However, 3 of 7 paid models introduced accuracy regressions not present in the free-tier baseline. google/gemma-3-27b-it (the mentor's recommendation), anthropic/claude-haiku-4.5, and mistralai/mistral-small-3.2-24b-instruct showed no regressions across any tested form.

Total spend across all paid-tier testing: approximately $0.30. Local and free-tier testing incurred no cost.

01

Where AI actually gets used

Every uploaded PDF is read by a multimodal AI model, which turns it into a list of plain questions and, where the form has real fillable fields, maps its own detections back onto them. Most of the rest of the app — moving between questions, saving your answers, producing the final document — needs no AI at all.

Pivot, 2026-08-03: this used to be two paths — real PDF fields were read locally with no AI involved, and only "flat" forms without fields went to a model. Local field-detection turned out to be unreliable across real-world PDFs (tested against 10-15 real forms), so every PDF now goes through the same model call. See the integration doc for the full reasoning. This means AI usage is no longer occasional — it happens on every upload, not just flat ones.
02

How the model is accessed: OpenRouter

Rather than signing up with one AI company directly, FormFill goes through OpenRouter, a single service that gives access to models from many different providers (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, and others) through one connection and one bill.

  • Flexibility: switching models is a one-line change in the code, not a new integration.
  • Price comparison in one place: every model's cost is visible side by side, so the cheapest option that still does the job well can be picked deliberately.
  • Free options exist: several capable models are available at no cost. This project currently uses one of them, described below.

04c

Third test round — trying the same models in the cloud

Everything above was run on Rahul's own laptop, which has no dedicated graphics card — that's why some of those runs took 15–20 minutes. This round asks a different question: if the same kind of model runs on OpenRouter's servers instead (rented, professional-grade hardware), how much faster does it get, and does it still read forms correctly?

In plain terms: a "model" here is the AI that looks at an uploaded form and works out what questions it's asking, so the app can read them aloud and let someone answer by voice or text. Two things matter for picking one: does it get the form right (no invented questions, no missed ones), and how fast/cheap is it to run for every person who uses the app. This section tracks both, in something closer to plain English than the technical tables above.

Test set so far: google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it, using its free tier (a version of Google's Gemma 4 model that OpenRouter hosts at no cost) — the closest cloud match to Nigel's own recommendation, which was tested locally in the second test cycle. More models and forms are being added to this table as tests continue.

04d

Fourth test round — a full 16-form stress test, three free cloud models

Once the first five cloud results looked promising, the natural next question was: does this hold up at real scale, uploaded as a batch, back to back, with no gaps in between? Two more free models were added for comparison at the same time — Google's slightly larger Gemma 4 31B, and a completely different model family from Nvidia (Nemotron), specifically built for reading documents rather than general chat. 16 real forms were uploaded together and run through all three models — 48 attempts total.

Headline result: only one of the three models actually worked: google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free, and even it started strong then slowed down partway through the batch before recovering by the end. The other two free models (gemma-4-31b-it:free and Nvidia's Nemotron) never once produced a usable result across all 16 forms — not because they read forms badly, but because neither ever got a real chance to finish: one was constantly told to slow down by its host, the other consistently ran out of time before answering.
Model Worked What went wrong the rest of the time In plain terms
google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free 6 / 16 8 timed out, 1 hit a shared file-reading outage, 1 tripped a bug in our own comparison tool (not the model's fault) The only model that worked at all, and every time it did succeed, the result was genuinely accurate — same quality bar as the earlier single-form tests. But it visibly slowed down and started timing out through the middle of this large back-to-back batch, then came right again on the very last form. This looks like the free tier getting busy under sustained heavy use, not a flaw in the model itself — a real person using the app one form at a time, not 16 in a row, would likely see far fewer of these failures.
google/gemma-4-31b-it:free 0 / 16 14 rejected instantly with "temporarily rate-limited," 1 hit the same file-reading outage, 1 tool bug This model's free capacity was essentially unavailable for the entire test, usually rejecting the request within 2–7 seconds with a message saying it was overloaded and to try again later. This says nothing about whether the model is any good at reading forms — it never actually got to try. Not a viable option right now, but not ruled out for the future either.
nvidia/nemotron-nano-12b-v2-vl:free 0 / 16 15 out of 16 ran out of time (2-minute safety limit) with no answer at all, 1 hit the shared file-reading outage Every single attempt either timed out or failed before reaching the model. Like the model above, this tells us nothing about actual accuracy — it never once produced a real answer to judge. Either this model is currently too slow to be practical on the free tier, or its free capacity is similarly overloaded.
A shared outage, not a model problem: one form ("Criminal history consent form") failed identically and instantly on all three models with the message "Failed to parse." A closer look at one of these errors showed OpenRouter uses a separate, shared document-reading service behind the scenes (from a company called Mistral) to turn a PDF into readable text before any model sees it — and that service was briefly down. This affected every model equally and had nothing to do with which AI was chosen.
A bug in our own testing tool, not the model: twice, gemma-4-26b returned an answer but our comparison tool crashed while trying to read it ("Cannot read properties of undefined"), because the tool assumed every answer would always include a list of form sections and didn't handle the rare case where that was missing. This has been fixed in scripts/online-test-lib.ts so future runs don't lose that data — but it means 2 of the 16 results for this model are unknown rather than pass/fail.

What this means for choosing a model: gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free remains the clear front-runner — it is the only model that has ever successfully read a real form in any test so far, local or cloud, and every time it succeeds the result is high quality. But this batch shows it isn't yet bulletproof under heavy back-to-back load, so it shouldn't be treated as "solved" — a smaller-scale real-world trial (one form at a time, like an actual user) is the fairer next test. The other two free models are not ruled out, but they need to be tried again on quieter days, or with a longer time limit, before any real verdict can be reached on their accuracy.

The fair way to test that theory is to try the exact same 10 failed forms again, individually this time, with real gaps between each — only gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free was used (the other two free models were dropped from routine testing, since neither ever produced a single result to judge).

A real pattern, not just bad luck: every single one of the 6 forms that failed twice (NZ citizenship, IR742, PAS310 passport, the sponsorship form, the childcare subsidy form, and the community services card) is an official New Zealand government form (Inland Revenue, Internal Affairs, Immigration NZ, or Work and Income). Every form that succeeded once retried alone was not a government form (ACC, a university scholarship, a real estate consent form, a defence force medal application, a tax code declaration). This is 6-for-6 vs. 3-for-3 — a strong enough split to treat as a genuine finding, not coincidence, though the exact reason (form length, layout density, something about how these particular PDFs are built) isn't confirmed yet.
A second, previously-hidden bug found and fixed: the residential tenancy agreement's "fetch failed" traced back to how Node's built-in network requests work. The testing tool (and, it turns out, the real production code that talks to OpenRouter) was using a default network setting that can silently cut off a slow-but-working request before it finishes, separate from the 2-minute limit this project intentionally sets. This has now been fixed in both the testing tool and the actual app code (src/lib/openrouter/client.ts) so this failure mode shouldn't happen again, in testing or for a real user.

What this means for choosing a model: gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free is confirmed reliable on ordinary forms — 6 real successes now across two rounds, zero failures on any non-government form. But it has a real, repeatable blind spot on New Zealand government forms specifically, which needs to be understood (and ideally fixed, e.g. with a longer timeout for these specific forms) before this model can be called demo-ready for a product whose whole point is helping people fill out exactly these kinds of forms.

06

The second model: answer verification

Since the pivot, a second, independent model call can review a filled-out form for genuine mistakes before submission — deliberately a different model from classification, so the same blind spot can't pass its own check (src/lib/openrouter/verify-answers.ts, /api/verify). This step is text-only (it reads field labels and typed answers, not the PDF itself), so it doesn't need vision, and can be a much cheaper model than classification.

Current target: nvidia/nemotron-3-super-120b-a12b:free, set in VERIFICATION_MODEL. Nigel's original suggestion was a free DeepSeek model, but no DeepSeek listing on OpenRouter is currently free (checked 2026-08-04 against the live models API — cheapest paid option is deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731 at $0.09/M input). Nemotron 3 Super is the closest substitute that still meets the actual requirement: free, a genuinely different model family from the classifier (Gemma), and large (120B) enough to be a real reasoning check rather than a token-saving shortcut.
Tested directly against /api/verify, 2026-08-04: two real requests. First, a name typed into an email field (a genuine mismatch) — correctly flagged with a clear, plain-language message. Second, three genuinely valid answers including a deliberately minimal "N/A" on an optional field — correctly returned no issues at all, matching the prompt's "flag sparingly, don't nitpick style" instruction. Now called from the real /confirm flow (handleFinish()) whenever a form is confirmed and downloaded — deliberately best-effort and not yet surfaced anywhere in the UI (a verification failure never blocks the download), so the result is available but there's no warning banner or gate built on it yet. That UI decision is left open for whoever picks it up next.
06b

Free vs. paid, head to head — 2026-08-05

Prompted by a real question: does paying for a model actually fix the free model's known gap on long/dense forms, and if it does, is the free model's accuracy still competitive, or is speed the only thing free is winning on? Speed and cost: every paid model here is 15–35× faster than the free model, at a cost of roughly 1–4 cents per form. This is the clearest, most unambiguous result — paid models genuinely solve the free model's biggest weakness (timing out on long/dense forms, confirmed separately on a 26-page form that failed 3 times on the free tier and succeeded in 5.3s with Gemini 2.5 Flash).
Accuracy is not simply "better because paid." Two real, repeatable problems showed up only in paid models, never in the free model across the same 5 forms:

Confidence calibration collapse. The whole point of a confidence score is flagging genuinely uncertain fields for a closer look (/review tags anything under 0.5). Three paid models — both Gemini variants and Qwen2.5-VL — returned the exact same confidence value for every field on at least one form, sometimes every form. That's not real calibration, it's a constant. If this ships, low-confidence flagging silently stops meaning anything for any form processed by one of these three.

Missed and mis-structured fields. gpt-4o-mini silently dropped 16 of 23 real questions on one form — no error, no low confidence, just gone. gemini-2.5-flash-lite split a single Yes/No question into two separate disconnected boolean fields (the same structural bug flagged earlier from a single-form test) — a real usability problem, since nothing stops someone answering "yes" to both halves.

Read after this comparison: claude-haiku-4.5, gemma-3-27b-it (Nigel's original pick), and mistral-small-3.2-24b are the three paid models that showed no accuracy regression against the free model across all 5 forms — all real confidence variation, zero structural bugs. Of those three, gemma-3-27b-it and mistral-small-3.2-24b are meaningfully cheaper per form than Claude Haiku. If switching to a paid model for the demo, one of those three is the evidence-backed choice — not simply "whichever is fastest or cheapest," since two of the cheapest/fastest options (the Gemini variants, Qwen) are exactly the ones with real accuracy problems.
