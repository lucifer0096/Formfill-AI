FormFill · AI model selection

Which AI model reads your forms, and what it costs

An overview of the model selection process behind form classification, and the tradeoffs between the available options.

00

Summary

Model testing proceeded in three phases: local inference on consumer hardware, free-tier cloud models, and paid cloud models. Each phase used real form PDFs, not synthetic test data. Full results and methodology for each phase are documented below; this table summarises the headline finding from each.

Phase Scope Result
1. Local (Ollama) 7 models narrowed to 4, evaluated on 2 real forms gemma3:4b, qwen2.5vl:7b, and gemma4:26b (the mentor's recommendation) all produced correct results. Consumer hardware without a dedicated GPU made this impractically slow for production (5 to 20 minutes per form).
2. Cloud, free tier 3 free OpenRouter models, evaluated on 16 real forms with individual retesting Only google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free proved reliable: fast (seconds per form) and accurate whenever it succeeded, with one confirmed limitation, consistent failures on official NZ government forms specifically. The other two free models never returned a usable result.
3. Cloud, paid tier 7 paid OpenRouter models, including the mentor's original recommendation, benchmarked against the free-tier result on 5 real forms Paid models were 15 to 35 times faster than local inference at a cost of a few cents per form, and confirmed to resolve the free tier's government-form limitation. However, 3 of 7 paid models introduced accuracy regressions not present in the free-tier baseline. google/gemma-3-27b-it (the mentor's recommendation), anthropic/claude-haiku-4.5, and mistralai/mistral-small-3.2-24b-instruct showed no regressions across any tested form.

Total spend across all paid-tier testing: approximately $0.30. Local and free-tier testing incurred no cost.

01

Where AI actually gets used

Every uploaded PDF is read by a multimodal AI model, which turns it into a list of plain questions and, where the form has real fillable fields, maps its own detections back onto them. Most of the rest of the app (moving between questions, saving answers, producing the final document) needs no AI at all.

Pivot. This used to be two paths: real PDF fields were read locally with no AI involved, and only "flat" forms without fields went to a model. Local field detection turned out to be unreliable across real-world PDFs, tested against 10 to 15 real forms, so every PDF now goes through the same model call. See the integration doc for the full reasoning. AI usage is no longer occasional; it happens on every upload, not just flat ones.
02

How the model is accessed: OpenRouter

Rather than signing up with one AI company directly, FormFill goes through OpenRouter, a single service that gives access to models from many different providers (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, and others) through one connection and one bill.

  • Flexibility: switching models is a one-line change in the code, not a new integration.
  • Price comparison in one place: every model's cost is visible side by side, so the cheapest option that still does the job well can be picked deliberately.
  • Free options exist: several capable models are available at no cost. This project currently uses one of them, described below.
03

What FormFill targets today

src/lib/openrouter/client.ts targets google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free for classification: free, and the only model validated as consistently accurate across real-form testing. It has one known limitation, detailed in section 4e below and in the paid-model comparison in section 6b.

Local test set, chosen to cover different sizes and architecture families: gemma3:4b, gemma4:e4b, gemma4:26b, qwen2.5vl:3b, qwen2.5vl:7b, llama3.2-vision:11b, and minicpm-v (a document-OCR specialist, included since reading a form is closer to OCR than general image captioning). The second-pass answer-verification step (section 6, "The second model: answer verification") is text-only and doesn't need vision; see that section for its own model choice.
04

First local test cycle

The full 7-model local set (npm run test:ui) was run against one real form, FHP-Statutory-Declaration-Form.pdf (a multi-page statutory declaration with mixed field types: text, choice, boolean, file-attachment references, a signature line). Hardware: Intel i7-10610U, 4C/8T mobile CPU, 32GB RAM, no usable GPU (Intel UHD, approximately 1GB VRAM); every model ran on CPU alone. Timeout was 20 minutes for this cycle, later raised to 60 minutes (see the note below).

Model Result Time Notes
gemma3:4b success 628s Only clean result. Correct title, 6 sensible sections, 13 fields with correct types (name, text, address, choice, date, signature). One cosmetic issue: stray formatting characters appended to several labels, the model echoing its own JSON punctuation into the label text.
qwen2.5vl:3b timed out 1200s (cap) Never finished. ollama ps confirmed it stayed loaded and active the whole time, not stuck. No verdict either way at this timeout length.
gemma4:e4b failed 307s Finished fast but returned a confidently wrong response claiming no PDF or image was received at all (confidence: 1 on a fabricated field). A correctness failure, not a speed one.
qwen2.5vl:7b timed out 1200s (cap) Same as the 3b version. No verdict at 20 minutes.
minicpm-v failed 870s Finished, but broke the response schema: invented a field type outside the allowed list, several bare string entries inside what should be an array of field objects, and full confidence on every field regardless of ambiguity. The prompt explicitly calls for honest low-confidence flags; this model wasn't calibrating at all.
llama3.2-vision:11b failed 1.7s Hard failure, not a hardware or timeout issue: this Ollama build doesn't support the model's architecture at all. Retesting would not help without an Ollama update.
gemma4:26b timed out 1200s (cap) The mentor's original recommendation. No verdict at 20 minutes; worth a retest at the extended timeout.
Timeout raised from 20 to 60 minutes after this cycle (scripts/model-test-lib.ts). Three of the seven models hit the 20-minute cap without a real result either way, confirmed still genuinely working via ollama ps, not stuck. A separate root-cause fix landed the same day: Node's global fetch is built on undici, whose default 300-second header timeout was silently failing every model taking longer than 5 minutes with a bare "fetch failed," before this file's own timeout logic ever got a chance to apply. Fixed by using undici's fetch directly with a custom agent.

Conclusion: narrow the next test round to gemma3:4b (confirmed baseline), qwen2.5vl:7b, and gemma4:26b (both unresolved, worth a retest at 60 minutes; the latter is the mentor's own pick). qwen2.5vl:3b, gemma4:e4b, minicpm-v, and llama3.2-vision:11b are deprioritised: the first three failed on correctness rather than just running out of time, and the last has a hard compatibility wall unrelated to timeout or prompting.

04b

Second local test cycle: ACC claim form

The shrunk 4-model set (gemma3:4b, gemma4:e4b, qwen2.5vl:7b, gemma4:26b) was run against a different real form, acc-form.pdf (a 3-page NZ ACC injury claim: mixed text, date, and choice fields, a conditional "previous claim" section, an authorisation and declaration block, and a signature line). Same hardware as the first cycle. Timeout: 60 minutes. All four models finished; no timeouts this time.

Model Result Time Notes
gemma3:4b success 313s Best result of the cycle. Correctly grouped all sections including the declaration, sensible help text on every field, high confidence throughout, no fabrication.
gemma4:e4b failed 310s Fabricated the entire form. Returned a fully formed but completely unrelated "Membership Enrollment Form" with high confidence scores; not a timeout or a refusal, a confident hallucination. The model's second correctness failure in a row on two different forms. Dropped from the rotation.
qwen2.5vl:7b success 878s Correct and thorough across all 3 pages, but roughly 3 times slower than gemma3:4b and missing help text on most fields. One field typed as "unknown" at confidence 0.5 rather than guessing; arguably better calibration, not a flaw.
gemma4:26b success 1183s (about 20 min) The mentor's pick. Correct and slightly more thorough section grouping, very high confidence (0.95 to 0.98) throughout. By far the slowest of the four; roughly 4 times gemma3:4b's time for a modest accuracy edge.
Conclusion: gemma4:e4b is out; two forms, two confident fabrications, worse than a slow-but-correct model or an honest failure. The remaining decision is gemma3:4b (fastest, accurate) versus gemma4:26b (the mentor's pick, marginally higher confidence, roughly 4 times slower) versus qwen2.5vl:7b (correct but slow, thinner on help text).
04c

Third test round: the same models in the cloud

Local testing ran on a laptop with no dedicated graphics card, which is why some runs took 15 to 20 minutes. This round tests whether the same model family, running on OpenRouter's infrastructure instead, is both faster and still accurate.

Context: two things matter in picking a model here: whether it gets the form right (no invented questions, none missed), and how fast and inexpensive it is to run for every user. This section tracks both.

Initial candidate: google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it, using its free tier, the closest cloud match to the mentor's own recommendation tested locally in the second cycle above.

Form tested Result Time Cost Notes
ACC injury claim form success 32 seconds $0 (free tier) Correctly found every section of the form and phrased each as a clear spoken question. The same model family took 20 minutes locally for a similar form; the cloud version was roughly 35 to 40 times faster.
First Home Partner statutory declaration success 93 seconds $0 (free tier) A genuinely hard form: dense legal paragraphs with multiple conditions bundled into single sentences. The model correctly broke each paragraph into a clear yes/no question, without inventing anything or missing a condition. The strongest result of any test to that point.
NZ Operational Service Medal application success 73 seconds $0 (free tier) A form where the person filling it in is not always the person the application concerns; the model kept the two identities correctly separated, told apart two conditional declaration sections, and honestly flagged lower confidence on a handwritten-style field rather than guessing.
Northland Pharmacist Pathway Scholarship application success 61 seconds $0 (free tier) Correctly identified conditional field requirements and added them as guidance rather than treating them as always-required. Correctly separated short structured questions from a longer essay-style question.
NZ citizenship application (adult) failed (twice) 2s, then 120s (timeout) — Failed twice in two different ways. First attempt: OpenRouter's own file handling rejected the PDF before the model saw it, in under 2 seconds. Second attempt: the file was accepted, but the model did not respond within the 2-minute limit. Two different failures on the same file suggested a genuine problem case, possibly related to length or density; flagged for follow-up testing with a paid model.
On the "$0" figures: OpenRouter's free tier does not charge; no funds had been loaded into the account and nothing was billed. An early version of this table briefly showed a small non-zero cost figure from the raw API response; this turned out to be a reference value some providers report even on free routes, not an actual charge, and has since been corrected.

This first wave (5 forms, one model) was strong. The next step was a larger stress test; see the fourth test round below.

04d

Fourth test round: a 16-form stress test, three free cloud models

The next question was whether the strong initial results held up at scale, uploaded as a batch with no gaps between forms. Two more free models were added for comparison: a slightly larger Gemma 4 variant, and a document-focused model from Nvidia (Nemotron). 16 real forms were run through all three models, 48 attempts in total.

Result: only one of the three models worked at all, google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free, and even it slowed through the middle of the batch before recovering by the end. The other two models never produced a single usable result across all 16 forms, not because of accuracy problems but because neither completed enough attempts to be judged: one was consistently rate-limited by its provider, the other consistently timed out.
Model Success rate Failure modes Notes
google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free 6 / 16 8 timed out, 1 hit a shared file-reading outage, 1 tripped a bug in the comparison tool The only model that worked at all, and accurate every time it did: the same quality bar as the earlier single-form tests. It visibly slowed and began timing out through the middle of this large batch, then recovered on the final form, consistent with free-tier congestion under sustained load rather than a model limitation.
google/gemma-4-31b-it:free 0 / 16 14 rejected instantly as rate-limited, 1 hit the shared outage, 1 tool bug Free capacity for this model was effectively unavailable throughout the test, typically rejecting requests within 2 to 7 seconds. This reflects availability, not accuracy; the model never got a real attempt.
nvidia/nemotron-nano-12b-v2-vl:free 0 / 16 15 of 16 timed out at the 2-minute limit, 1 hit the shared outage Every attempt either timed out or failed before reaching the model. As with the model above, this says nothing about accuracy; no real answer was ever produced to evaluate.
A shared infrastructure outage, not a model problem: one form failed identically and instantly on all three models. The error detail showed OpenRouter uses a separate, shared document pre-processing service (from Mistral) to convert a PDF into readable content before any model sees it, and that service was briefly unavailable. This affected every model equally and was unrelated to model choice.
A bug in the comparison tool, not the model: twice, gemma-4-26b returned a valid answer, but the comparison tool crashed while reading it, because it assumed every response would include a populated sections array and did not handle the case where that was missing. Fixed in scripts/online-test-lib.ts; 2 of the 16 results for this model were recorded as unknown rather than pass or fail as a result.

Conclusion: gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free remained the clear front-runner, the only model to have successfully read a real form in any test to that point, with consistently high quality when it succeeded. The batch showed it was not yet reliable under heavy concurrent load, so a smaller-scale, more realistic trial (one form at a time) was identified as the fairer next test. The other two free models were not ruled out on accuracy, since neither was given a fair opportunity to demonstrate it.

04e

Fifth test round: retrying the 10 failures individually

The fourth round's working theory was that the model itself was sound but had been overwhelmed running 16 forms back to back. To test that theory directly, the same 10 failed forms were retried individually, with real gaps between attempts, using only gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free (the other two free models were dropped from routine testing, having never produced a single result to evaluate).

Form Result Notes
Medical Examiner designation application succeeded Timed out in the batch; worked cleanly alone (114s). Supports the batch-congestion theory.
Criminal history consent form succeeded Hit the shared parsing outage in the batch; worked cleanly alone (106s), confirming the earlier failure was the outage, not this file.
IR330 (tax code declaration) succeeded Previously hit the comparison tool's bug rather than a real model failure. Now returns a fast (55s), accurate result.
NZ citizenship application (adult) failed again Timed out a second time, this time in isolation, ruling out batch congestion. The longest form tested (26 pages) and an official NZ government form.
IR742 (offshore IRD number application) failed again Timed out a second time in isolation. Only 4 pages, so length alone does not explain it. Another NZ government form.
PAS310 (NZ passport application) failed again Timed out a second time in isolation. Another NZ government form.
Sponsorship Form for Temporary Entry (INZ 1025) failed again Timed out a second time in isolation. A 13-page NZ Immigration form.
Childcare and OSCAR subsidy application failed again First real attempt, having previously hit the tool bug; still timed out in isolation. A Work and Income (MSD) form.
Community Services Card application failed again Timed out a second time in isolation. Another Work and Income (MSD) form.
Residential tenancy agreement failed differently Previously hit the tool bug; this attempt failed with a network-level error after 76 seconds, traced to a gap in the testing tool's request configuration (since fixed). Not a government form, but similar in legal density.
A genuine pattern, not coincidence: every one of the 6 forms that failed twice (NZ citizenship, IR742, PAS310, the sponsorship form, the childcare subsidy form, and the community services card) is an official New Zealand government form (Inland Revenue, Internal Affairs, Immigration NZ, or Work and Income). Every form that succeeded once retried alone was not a government form. A 6-for-6 versus 3-for-3 split is a strong enough signal to treat as a genuine finding, though the exact cause (form length, layout density, or something specific to how these PDFs are constructed) has not been confirmed.
A second, previously hidden bug, found and fixed: the residential tenancy agreement's network error traced back to a Node.js default network timeout setting that could silently cut off a slow-but-working request, independent of this project's own intended 2-minute limit. Fixed in both the testing tool and the production client code (src/lib/openrouter/client.ts).

Conclusion: gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free is confirmed reliable on ordinary forms, with 6 successes across two rounds and zero failures on any non-government form. It has a real, repeatable limitation on New Zealand government forms specifically, addressed further in section 6b below.

06

The second model: answer verification

A second, independent model call reviews a filled-out form for genuine mistakes before submission, deliberately a different model from classification so the same blind spot cannot pass its own check (src/lib/openrouter/verify-answers.ts, /api/verify). This step is text-only, reading field labels and typed answers rather than the PDF itself, so it does not need vision and can use a much cheaper model than classification.

Current target: the free-tier nvidia/nemotron-3-super-120b-a12b:free, set in VERIFICATION_MODEL. A paid model, anthropic/claude-haiku-4.5, was used for hackathon-day reliability, matching the same switch made to the classification model; now that the hackathon is over, back to the free tier. Genuinely different model family from the classifier either way, keeping the actual point of a second, independent check intact. If paid reliability is needed again, Claude Haiku was one of the models validated with no accuracy regression in the head-to-head comparison above.
Tested directly against /api/verify: two real requests. First, a name typed into an email field, a genuine mismatch, correctly flagged with a clear message. Second, three genuinely valid answers including a deliberately minimal response on an optional field, correctly returned with no issues, matching the prompt's instruction to flag sparingly rather than nitpick style. Called from the production confirm flow whenever a form is confirmed and downloaded, deliberately best-effort: a verification failure never blocks the download. If it flags a real issue, a non-blocking warning banner on the confirm page shows it in plain language.
06b

Free versus paid: head-to-head comparison

This comparison was prompted by two questions: does paying for a model resolve the free model's known limitation on long or dense forms, and if so, does the free model's accuracy remain competitive, or is speed the only advantage of paying? google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free (already validated) was benchmarked against 7 paid candidates, all 8 models on the same 5 real forms.

Total cost: $0.21 across 35 paid calls (7 models times 5 forms). Estimated at approximately $0.35 beforehand; came in under estimate.
Model Avg. time (5 forms) Total cost (5 forms) Confidence calibration Structural bugs found
google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it:free 90.2s $0 Genuine variation: 2 to 4 distinct confidence values on every form None, across 128 fields checked
anthropic/claude-haiku-4.5 7.7s $0.0714 Genuine variation: 3 to 4 distinct values on every form, the most consistent of any paid model None
google/gemma-3-27b-it (mentor's pick) 3.6s $0.0280 Genuine variation: 1 to 5 distinct values, mostly good None
mistralai/mistral-small-3.2-24b-instruct 3.4s $0.0268 Genuine variation: 2 to 3 distinct values consistently None
google/gemini-2.5-flash 2.8s $0.0268 Weak: collapsed to a single confidence value on 2 of 5 forms None
openai/gpt-4o-mini 2.8s $0.0202 Genuine variation: 2 to 5 distinct values Yes: returned only 7 of 23 real fields on the criminal history consent form, missing roughly 70 percent of the form
google/gemini-2.5-flash-lite 3.3s $0.0049 Weak: collapsed to a single value on 3 of 5 forms, worse than the full Flash model Yes: split a single yes/no question into two disconnected boolean fields on the ACC form
qwen/qwen2.5-vl-72b-instruct 4.3s $0.0317 Broken: a single confidence value, no variation at all, on every one of the 5 forms None
Speed and cost: every paid model tested was 15 to 35 times faster than the free model, at a cost of roughly 1 to 4 cents per form. This is the clearest and most unambiguous result: paid models resolve the free model's primary weakness, timing out on long or dense forms, confirmed separately on a 26-page form that failed three times on the free tier and succeeded in 5.3 seconds with Gemini 2.5 Flash.
Accuracy is not simply better because a model is paid. Two real, repeatable problems appeared only in paid models, never in the free model, across the same 5 forms.

Confidence calibration collapse. A confidence score exists to flag genuinely uncertain fields for closer review (the review page tags anything under 0.5). Three paid models, both Gemini variants and Qwen2.5-VL, returned the exact same confidence value for every field on at least one form, sometimes every form. That is not calibration, it is a constant, and it would silently defeat low-confidence flagging on any form processed by one of these three.

Missed and mis-structured fields. gpt-4o-mini silently dropped 16 of 23 real questions on one form, with no error and no low-confidence flag. gemini-2.5-flash-lite split a single yes/no question into two separate, disconnected boolean fields, the same structural issue observed in an earlier single-form test, a real usability problem since nothing prevents a user from answering yes to both halves.

Conclusion: claude-haiku-4.5, gemma-3-27b-it (the mentor's original recommendation), and mistral-small-3.2-24b showed no accuracy regression against the free model across any of the 5 forms tested, with genuine confidence variation and zero structural bugs. Of the three, gemma-3-27b-it and mistral-small-3.2-24b are meaningfully cheaper per form than Claude Haiku. If moving to a paid model, one of these three is the evidence-based choice; the cheapest or fastest options in this comparison were also the ones with real accuracy problems.

07

Switching classification models

Changing the classification model is a one-line edit in src/lib/openrouter/client.ts (CLASSIFICATION_MODEL). No further changes are needed; classify-pdf.ts and the prompt itself make no assumptions specific to any one model beyond accepting a PDF file and returning JSON.
